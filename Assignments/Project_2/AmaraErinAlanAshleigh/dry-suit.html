Dry Suit

Automated Pressurizer and Sonar Technology

Human to Adaptive Merfolk Technology
Two part suits designed for deep water exploration and communication. Mimics Merfolk anatomy for easy movement in water. Quick tech adaptability for changes in water pressure for survivability. New tech tail that pulls oxygen from water for a longer dive time.
Suit overview
The dry suit is split into two main pieces: the pressure adjusting suit and the oxygen converting tail. It also has a HUD monitor installed that displays the user's vitals for safety.

  • The shape of the suit mimics merfolk anatomy to help with underwater navigation. The suit allows humans to swim at higher speeds. Webbed fingers and flippers allow them to reach these speeds, and the dorsal fin allows humans to stay upright.
  • Due to vocal differences between humans and merfolk, an AI translator is needed for communication. Humans can speak into their translator, and through the quick translator can be turned into sonar clicks and whistles that are played through the speaker on their chest. The "eyes" of the suit act like a receptor for merfolk talking, able to "hear" the clicks and whistles and be translated into a human spoken language.

  • The tail exists as a separate piece to the suit as an attachment. Tubes run from the front of the suit to the back, all the way to the bottom of the tail. The tail extends all the way to the top of the back with a mock spine, and the dorsal fin sticking out. A metal endoskeleton starts at the top of the tail for movement. The tail is programmed to move constantly, not only for oxygen conversion but to keep the wearer upright and moving.
  • New technology developments and collaboration have introduced a system that pulls water in through the bottom of the tail, pulls the oxygen out of it and pushes it through the length of the tail to the head. An emergency air tank is inside the tail in case of blockage. Bubbles of carbon dioxide push out through the tail fins.
